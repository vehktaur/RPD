\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Corrosion is the deterioration of a metal through electrochemical interaction
with its environment \citep{Alvani2026}, as the metal returns to a more stable,
oxidized state \citep{Elizalde2023}. Its annual cost is usually put at 3--4\% of
gross domestic product (GDP) for most nations, 15--35\% of it avoidable and much
of it spent on inspection \citep{Nash2022}; globally, about US\$2.5 trillion a
year \citep{Imran2025}. In China in 2014 it was about 2127.8 billion RMB (US\$310
billion), 3.34\% of GDP \citep{Hou2017}. The burden is heaviest in energy and
shipping: corrosion causes nearly 27\% of the gas pipeline incidents recorded by
the European Gas Pipeline Incident Data Group \citep{Akhlaghi2023}, costs oil
tankers about US\$969 million a year \citep{Imran2025}, and can lead to leaks,
fires, explosions and pollution of soil and sea \citep{Akhlaghi2023,Alvani2026}.

Uniform corrosion removes metal evenly, so what is visible broadly reflects what
is lost. Pitting concentrates attack where the protective film breaks down,
forming deep wells of small diameter \citep{Soares2023}, and is regarded as more
dangerous because it is harder to detect \citep{Soares2023}: a surface that looks
sound can hide deep local loss, with pit depths of 0.41 to 13.44\,mm in field data
from buried pipelines \citep{Akhlaghi2023}. Depth therefore matters to safe
operation as well as area \citep{Akhlaghi2023,Huang2026a}.

Inspection is still mostly manual and visual, supported where access permits by
ultrasonic, radiographic and magnetic particle testing, which are time-consuming,
costly and dependent on skilled personnel \citep{Alvani2026}; visual inspection is
also subjective and inconsistent \citep{Espinoza2026,Savino2025}. Access makes it
worse: a full inspection of the tanks of a floating production, storage and
offloading (FPSO) vessel takes ten to fifteen days in confined, unhealthy spaces
\citep{Soares2023}.

Deep learning on photographs now detects, segments and grades corrosion on
pipelines, ships, bridges and towers \citep{Nash2022,Fu2024,Alvani2026,Savino2025},
and a UAV can carry the camera to structures that are hazardous or slow to reach
\citep{Savino2025,Nooralishahi2022}. A color camera, however, records appearance
only. Rust can resemble paint, shadows and scratches, and red, orange and brown
coatings resemble iron oxide in color \citep{Espinoza2026,Lavadiya2022};
corrosion under intact coating is invisible to it
\citep{Elizalde2023,Hernndez-Surez2026}; salt spray, humidity and changing light
degrade the image \citep{Mao2026}; and severity is estimated as the fraction of
corroded pixels, which does not measure material loss or depth
\citep{Alvani2026}.

Other sensors see what a camera misses. Thermography responds to defects beneath
the surface through their effect on heat flow, and has revealed corrosion under
paint and coatings \citep{Elizalde2023,Lim2021,Doshvarpassand2022}. LiDAR measures
surface geometry at a distance, and has been used to assess section loss in steel
members \citep{Maru2025} and to map corrosion in 3D
\citep{PimenteldeFigueiredo2025}. Each sensor has weaknesses, but fused sensors
offset one another's \citep{Tran2024}. This research fuses RGB, thermal and LiDAR
data in one detection framework for corrosion.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Camera-based detection mistakes look-alikes for corrosion, cannot see beneath
paint and mostly reports affected area rather than depth of loss
\citep{Alvani2026,Savino2025}; the few contactless depth estimates are made on
prepared specimens under controlled conditions
\citep{Egodawela2025,Doshvarpassand2022,Haitz2022,Maru2025}. Multi-sensor work on
corrosion is limited. Of the 33 corrosion-sensing studies reviewed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:literature-review}, 16 use RGB alone and five combine sensors,
only one of which adds thermal imaging \citep{Lim2021}; none fuses RGB, thermal and
LiDAR. Of the 40 fusion studies reviewed, the three that combine all three sensors
address driving or calibration, not corrosion \citep{Tran2025,Tran2024,Yang2024}.
Where a second sensor has been added for corrosion, its value is rarely measured:
\citet{Haitz2022} found laser data only marginally better than color images alone
and asked whether the extra sensor justifies the fusion it requires.

The problem is therefore twofold: no reviewed study fuses RGB, thermal and LiDAR
data to detect corrosion and measure its metal loss, and none measures what each
of the three sensors contributes to detection.

\subsection*{Hypothesis}

RGB, thermal and LiDAR sensors fail under different conditions \citep{Tran2024}.
A camera is degraded by shadow, poor light and salt spray
\citep{Soares2023,Mao2026,Espinoza2026} and can mistake rust-colored paint for
corrosion \citep{Espinoza2026,Lavadiya2022}. Thermal imaging sees defects below
the surface \citep{Elizalde2023,Doshvarpassand2022,Hernndez-Surez2026} but is
affected by sunlight, emissivity and, in passive use, weather
\citep{Elizalde2023,Egodawela2025}, and by reflective surfaces \citep{Tran2024}.
LiDAR measures shape but records no color, temperature or subsurface damage
\citep{Ding2025}, and its point clouds are sparse \citep{Tran2024}. Because no two
fail in the same way, it is hypothesized that a model fusing all three will detect
corrosion more accurately, on mAP@0.5:0.95, than any single sensor, by more than
the decision-rule margin of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, and that the LiDAR will
allow each detected region's metal loss to be measured as well as its area. If the
fused model is no better than the best single sensor, the hypothesis is rejected
and that result reported.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

\begin{enumerate}[label=RQ\arabic*.,leftmargin=*,itemsep=8pt]
  \item How can RGB, thermal and LiDAR data of corroded metal surfaces be
        registered to a common frame with pixel-level accuracy, when thermal and
        LiDAR data share little visual appearance with RGB?
  \item Does a model fusing registered RGB, thermal and LiDAR data detect
        corroded regions more accurately than the same model given RGB alone,
        and how accurately can each region's area and metal loss be measured
        from the LiDAR?
  \item What does each sensor contribute to detection when single-sensor,
        paired and full configurations, with a blank-channel control, are trained
        and evaluated under identical conditions?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Aim and Objectives}
\label{sec:aim-objectives}

The aim is to develop and evaluate a machine learning framework that detects
corroded regions on metal surfaces by fusing RGB, thermal and LiDAR data, measures
each region's area and metal loss from the LiDAR, and determines what each sensor
contributes to detection. The objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=6pt]
  \item To develop a pipeline that aligns RGB, thermal and LiDAR data of metal
        surfaces in a common reference frame.
  \item To develop a fusion-based deep learning model that detects corroded
        regions in the aligned data, and to measure each region's area and metal
        loss from the LiDAR.
  \item To evaluate each sensor's contribution through an ablation of
        single-sensor, RGB--thermal, RGB--LiDAR and full configurations, with a
        blank-channel control, under identical conditions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Significance and Contribution of Research}
\label{sec:significance}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=5pt]
  \item \textbf{A registered RGB--thermal--LiDAR corrosion dataset.} Without a
        public dataset, models are trained on local data and do not generalize
        \citep{Nash2022}. Public corrosion datasets now exist
        \citep{Espinoza2026}, but those identified are RGB-only. The dataset will
        be released if the owners of the scanned structures permit.
  \item \textbf{Contactless detection and measurement.} One fused model locates
        each corroded region, and the LiDAR gives its area and metal loss in
        physical units. No reviewed study does both with the three sensors.
  \item \textbf{A controlled ablation of what each sensor adds.} A blank-channel
        control separates what a sensor contributes from the effect of a larger
        input. This answers, for contactless sensors, the question
        \citet{Haitz2022} left open: whether an extra sensor is worth its cost,
        including when it is not.
\end{enumerate}

For practice, the outcome shows which sensors are worth deploying, and adds a
physical measure of metal loss to the area camera inspection already reports.

\section{Scope of Study}
\label{sec:scope}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=5pt]
  \item Only contactless optical sensing is used: RGB, thermal infrared and
        LiDAR. Ultrasonic, eddy current and magnetic flux methods are reviewed
        as context only.
  \item Thermal imaging is chosen over hyperspectral imaging
        (Section~\ref{sec:lr-modalities}).
  \item Detection places bounding boxes in a single class; pixel-level
        segmentation is not attempted.
  \item Severity is the area and metal loss of each detected region from the
        LiDAR, in physical units; volume and full 3D reconstruction are not
        attempted.
  \item The substrate and service environment will follow the data that can be
        acquired.
  \item Real-time adaptive learning, corrosion prevention and the prediction of
        corrosion rate or remaining life are excluded.
  \item The trimodal corrosion dataset is built in this research, starting with
        controlled specimens; if field structures cannot be accessed in time,
        evaluation remains on the specimens (Section~\ref{sec:acquisition}).
\end{enumerate}
