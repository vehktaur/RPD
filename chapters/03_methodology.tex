\section{Research Design}
\label{sec:research-design}

This research is quantitative and experimental. Its hypothesis
(Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement}) is tested by a controlled comparison:
detectors that differ only in the sensor data they receive, trained and evaluated
under identical conditions, with the metrics and decision rule fixed before any
model is trained.

The objectives form a chain. Objective~1 produces the registered data without
which no fused model can be trained, Objective~2 the detector and the per-region
measurements, and Objective~3 the comparison, the only one whose outcome is open:
a finding that a sensor adds nothing is a valid answer.

Two design rules follow from Chapter~\ref{ch:literature-review}. First, every
channel comes from a physical sensor; a channel computed from another, such as the
texture map of \citet{Mao2026}, is not a second sensor. Second, fusion takes place
at the input. Early fusion did best in both reviewed comparisons of fusion levels
and has been used for corrosion with real sensors (Section~\ref{sec:lr-fusion}),
and it keeps the ablation clean: every configuration uses the same network and
only the input channels change. Its cost is that the sensors must be registered
at the pixel level, which is why registration is an objective in its own right.

\section{Proposed Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

In the proposed framework (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}), the RGB image (three
channels), the thermal image (one) and a depth image projected from the LiDAR
point cloud (one) are registered to a common view and stacked into a five-channel
image. One YOLO11 detector \citep{Ultralytics2023} places a bounding box around
each corroded region, and the LiDAR data inside each box give its corroded area
in real units and its metal loss relative to the surrounding surface.

YOLO11 is chosen because YOLO detectors are the most common family in the
reviewed detection studies (Section~\ref{sec:lr-detection}) and in the
box-based corrosion detectors reviewed \citep{Mao2026,Fu2024,Bui2026,Li2024b,Yu2023},
are fast enough to run on a drone's own computer \citep{Yu2023}, and have already
been trained on RGB, depth and thermal images fused at the pixel level
\citep{Cao2026}. The five-channel input requires only a wider first
convolutional layer. Real-time deployment on the drone is not an objective.

\subsection{Data Acquisition}
\label{sec:acquisition}

No public dataset pairs RGB, thermal and LiDAR data of corroded metal
(Section~\ref{sec:lr-corrosion-detection}), so acquisition is part of this
research and its largest single risk. The sensors will be carried by a UAV; the
RGB and thermal cameras are available and the LiDAR is still to be selected. The
three will be mounted rigidly on one frame, as in the drone-based inspection of
\citet{Nooralishahi2022}, so that their relative positions are calibrated once.

Data will be collected first from controlled corrosion specimens, whose
corroded area and metal loss can be measured by hand, and then from field
structures if access is secured. A first study needs little data:
\citet{Lim2021} published an RGB--thermal corrosion result from 36 images. If
field access is not secured in time, evaluation will rely on the specimens, and
the limit on generalization will be reported, since models trained on data from
one source generalize poorly \citep{Nash2022}.

Two acquisition questions decide whether the method transfers to the field.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Thermal excitation.} The reviewed thermographic results on
        corrosion were obtained under deliberate thermal excitation: halogen
        lamps at a standoff of 1~m \citep{Lim2021}, a 1000~W quartz-tube heater
        \citep{Elizalde2023} and a freezing spray \citep{Doshvarpassand2022}.
        Whether passive thermal imaging from a UAV reveals the same corrosion is
        not established, so both regimes will be recorded on the same
        specimens where possible.
  \item \textbf{Timing.} Under active excitation, the thermal measurement is a
        sequence lasting until the surface returns to ambient temperature
        \citep{Doshvarpassand2022}, whereas RGB and LiDAR captures are single
        frames, so the protocol will fix which thermal frame is paired with them.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Calibration and Registration}
\label{sec:registration}

A printed checkerboard, the usual target for camera and LiDAR calibration, has
no thermal contrast, so two routes will be tried. The first is a calibration
target that all three sensors can see, such as the self-heating board of
\citet{Nooralishahi2022}, which uses Peltier modules to produce a sharp pattern
in both thermal and visible images. The second, if the target route fails, is
targetless calibration of the thermal, RGB and LiDAR triple; \citet{Yang2024}
report translation errors of 2.77~cm and 3.86~cm and rotation errors of
0.21\textdegree\ and 0.46\textdegree\ for the LiDAR--RGB and LiDAR--thermal pairs
respectively, and \citet{Tran2024} calibrate the same three sensors without
targets while moving.

After calibration, the LiDAR point cloud is projected onto the RGB image plane as
the depth image, and the thermal image is warped into the same view. Registration
accuracy is reported for Objective~1 as the residual offset in pixels between
corresponding features (see Section~\ref{sec:preliminary-results} for why this
matters).

\subsection{Preprocessing and Annotation}
\label{sec:annotation}

The target is a bounding box around each corroded region, in a single class.
Boxes are used instead of pixel masks because a pixel-level training set is
labor-intensive to build \citep{Lim2021}, and because box mAP is the metric of the
detection and fusion literature, so results can be compared with published work.

Each channel is normalized separately, since the RGB, thermal and LiDAR values
have unrelated scales. Hue and saturation augmentation is disabled in every
configuration, because it is meaningless for thermal and depth channels and
applying it to some arms only would bias the ablation.

The area and metal loss measurements need no extra labels. Because a box also
covers clean metal, the corroded pixels inside it are separated with the
rule-based color method of \citet{Alvani2026}, which combines the Lab $a^*$
channel, a red--green index and an HSV rust map and reached a mean IoU of 0.742
against manual masks, close to the 0.751 of a trained U-Net. A few areas will be
measured by hand to state the error of this step.

\subsection{Model Development}
\label{sec:model}

The detector is trained in three steps: from YOLO11 weights pretrained on COCO,
which teach general features and box placement; then fine-tuned on public RGB
corrosion images, too few to train a detector alone but enough to adapt one; and
finally, with the first layer widened from three to five channels, on the
registered trimodal data, with the RGB channels keeping their pretrained weights
and the thermal and depth channels starting from new ones.

Transfer learning is used because the trimodal dataset will be small. On steel
pipe pile corrosion, pretrained weights gave faster convergence and significantly
higher accuracy \citep{Han2024}; training from scratch on limited data risks
overfitting \citep{Rashidi2026}; and the LiDAR--image study of section loss used a
pretrained network for the same reason \citep{Maru2025}. Pretraining on RGB favours
the RGB channels, so any gain from thermal or LiDAR data is a conservative
estimate. Training from scratch is the fallback if the widened model trains
poorly.

Area and metal loss are measured from the LiDAR, not learned: the range to the
surface converts the count of corroded pixels in a box into a physical area, and
the depth inside the box, compared with the surrounding surface, gives the metal
loss. The smallest measurable loss is set by the precision of the LiDAR chosen.

\subsection{Ablation Protocol}
\label{sec:ablation}

The ablation is fixed before any corrosion result exists, so the reported
comparison cannot be chosen after the fact.

\textbf{Configurations.} Each sensor alone (RGB, thermal, LiDAR depth), RGB with
thermal, RGB with LiDAR depth, and all three. The single-sensor arms show whether
one sensor suffices; the paired arms isolate what thermal and LiDAR add to RGB.

\textbf{Blank-channel control.} One further arm uses the fused input with the
channel under test left empty, so that a gain due only to the extra input
capacity is not credited to the sensor.

\textbf{Held constant.} Architecture, pretrained weights, optimizer, learning-rate
schedule, epochs, early stopping, batch size, image size, augmentation and data
split. The data are split by specimen or structure, not by image, so frames of
one corroded region cannot appear in both training and test sets.

\textbf{Repeats.} Three random seeds per arm; results are the mean with the
observed range.

\textbf{Decision rule.} A sensor earns its place only if the arm containing it
beats the best arm without it, on mean mAP@0.5:0.95, by more than the largest
between-seed spread in any arm, so that a difference smaller than the
experiment's own noise is not reported as an effect.

Formally, with $m_{a,k}$ be the test mAP@0.5:0.95 of arm $a$ trained with seed
$k$ and $\bar{m}_a$ its mean over seeds, sensor $s$, tested in arm $a_s$, is
retained only if
\begin{equation}
\bar{m}_{a_s} - \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}_{\setminus s}} \bar{m}_a \;>\;
\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \Bigl( \max_k m_{a,k} - \min_k m_{a,k} \Bigr),
\label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of all arms and $\mathcal{A}_{\setminus s}$ the
arms that do not contain $s$.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Every configuration is compared with the RGB-only arm, the baseline a fused
detector must beat to be worth its cost, on precision, recall, mAP@0.5 and
mAP@0.5:0.95. Both mAP thresholds are reported because thermal and LiDAR data may
improve where a box is placed more than whether a region is found: mAP@0.5 counts
a box as correct at half overlap, whereas mAP@0.5:0.95 rewards boxes that fit
closely. \citet{Lim2021}, the closest published RGB--thermal corrosion result, is
a qualitative reference only, since its data, structures, detector and excitation
differ. The LiDAR area and metal loss are compared with hand measurements on a
subset of regions, and their errors reported in physical units.

For a predicted box $B_p$ and a labelled box $B_g$, the intersection over union is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IoU}(B_p,B_g) = \frac{\lvert B_p \cap B_g \rvert}{\lvert B_p \cup B_g \rvert}.
\label{eq:iou}
\end{equation}
A prediction is a true positive (TP) when its IoU with an unmatched label
reaches a threshold $\tau$, and a false positive (FP) otherwise; an unmatched
label is a false negative (FN). Then
\begin{equation}
P = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \qquad R = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}.
\label{eq:pr}
\end{equation}
Sweeping the confidence threshold traces a precision--recall curve whose area
is the average precision,
$\mathrm{AP}_\tau = \int_0^1 P(R)\,\mathrm{d}R$. With one class, mAP equals AP, so
\begin{equation}
\text{mAP@0.5} = \mathrm{AP}_{0.5}, \qquad
\text{mAP@0.5:0.95} = \frac{1}{10} \sum_{\tau \in \{0.50,\,0.55,\,\ldots,\,0.95\}} \mathrm{AP}_\tau .
\label{eq:map}
\end{equation}
AP is computed with the Ultralytics implementation \citep{Ultralytics2023}, which,
as in the COCO benchmark, interpolates $P(R)$ at 101 evenly spaced recall points.

\section{Preliminary Results}
\label{sec:preliminary-results}

With no public trimodal corrosion dataset, the pipeline was tested on a public
trimodal dataset from another domain, to show that it and the ablation protocol
run end to end and give an interpretable verdict.

\subsection{Dataset and Setup}

MID-1K \citep{Mota2024} is a person-detection dataset of 1,100 frames recorded
from a Clearpath Jackal ground robot with an RGB camera (Ximea), a thermal camera
(FLIR Boson 640) and a 64-beam LiDAR (Ouster OS1-64) projected onto the RGB image
plane as a depth image. The authors' 397 test frames (1,629 labelled people) were
kept as the test set; the rest were split into 591 training and 112 validation
frames, the validation frames held out as whole recording sessions.

Every arm used YOLO11n pretrained on COCO, the same split and the same recipe:
100 epochs with early stopping after 20 without improvement, 640-pixel images,
batch size 16, hue and saturation augmentation disabled, and three seeds. Because
the pretrained detector expects three channels, the fused arm stacked grayscale
RGB with the thermal and depth images. Table~\ref{tab:prelim-arms} lists the six
arms, and Figure~\ref{fig:prelim-input} shows the fused input for one test frame.

Early fusion did not satisfy the decision rule (Equation~\ref{eq:decision}): on
mAP@0.5:0.95 it reached 0.324 against 0.311 for RGB, a gain of 0.013, while the
largest between-seed spread was about 0.05. It also scored the same as the blank
control with the depth channel empty, so depth added nothing beyond grayscale and
thermal here. Depth alone was the weakest arm.

Thermal scored 0.756 mAP@0.5 against labels drawn on the thermal image but 0.521
against the RGB labels, a loss of nearly a third. MID-1K's thermal camera is only
approximately registered to the RGB view (Figure~\ref{fig:misalign}): matching
each RGB label box to the nearest thermal one gives a median center offset of
about 5 pixels, with 90\% of pairs within 13, a figure that includes differences
in how the two label sets were drawn as well as camera misalignment. Because the
fused input is scored against RGB labels, its thermal channel partly points at
the wrong place, a likely reason fusion could not exploit it.

Every arm scored much higher on validation than on test, most likely because the

\subsection{What the Preliminary Results Establish}

Three lessons carry over. First, the pipeline runs end to end: six arms and
three seeds were trained and scored with one recipe, and reruns with the same
seed reproduced the results. Second, the blank-channel control behaved as
intended: fusion scored no higher than the same input with depth left empty.
Third, registration appears to have to come first: loose alignment, together with
differences between the label sets, cost thermal nearly a third of its score on
the RGB boxes, which is why registration is Objective~1.

The test says nothing about corrosion: the class is people, the detector is the
smallest YOLO11, and COCO weights, which include a person class, favour RGB. Its
value is in showing that the protocol is ready for corrosion data.
