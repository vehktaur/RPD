\begin{abstract}
    Pitting corrosion is among the most dangerous degradation mechanisms affecting offshore infrastructure: it forms concealed, localised cavities that penetrate deep into metal while evading routine visual inspection. In current offshore practice, structural assessment of topside assets and vessel hulls relies predominantly on manual visual inspection. This costly, subjective process requires inspectors to spend prolonged periods in confined spaces. Automated alternatives proposed in research remain largely single-modality, relying chiefly on RGB imagery, which is unreliable in maritime conditions and cannot capture subsurface anomalies or pit geometry. The fusion of RGB, thermal, and laser profilometry has recently proven effective for pitting characterisation, but only in controlled industrial environments and using localised 2D profiling; no existing study extends trimodal fusion to offshore infrastructure. This research builds upon that proven multimodal strategy, extending it to exposed offshore surfaces through drone-deployable LiDAR sensing, using transfer-learned RGB and thermal encoders with a custom fusion design that integrates RGB and thermal features with 3D LiDAR geometry. The methodology encompasses multimodal data acquisition and co-registration, transfer learning of visual backbones, custom fusion design, and systematic evaluation against single- and dual-modality baselines. The expected outcome is a validated trimodal framework for automated pitting detection and severity characterisation, offering improved robustness in offshore conditions alongside quantified evidence of each sensing modality's contribution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Offshore infrastructure such as oil and gas platforms, floating production storage and offloading (FPSO) vessels, subsea pipelines, and marine support structure must maintain structural rigidity and integrity under some of the harshest operating conditions. To achieve the required durability and longevity, these structures are constructed predominantly from high-performance metals such as stainless and structural steels, selected for their strength and resistance to the saline, high-humidity, and mechanically demanding marine environment \cite{dalmora2025methods}.

Despite this deliberate material selection, offshore metallic structures remain inherently prone to degradation over their service life. The principal degradation mechanism is corrosion. Corrosion is an electrochemical and chemical process in which metal deteriorates as a result of progressive oxidation through reactions with its surrounding environment \cite{balangao2024corrosion}. In marine settings, the combination of dissolved salts, oxygen availability, temperature cycling, and biological activity substantially accelerates these reactions, making offshore assets among the most corrosion-vulnerable structures in industrial service \cite{dalmora2025methods}.

Among the various forms of corrosion, pitting corrosion is particularly insidious. Unlike uniform corrosion, which thins a surface evenly and predictably, pitting concentrates attack into small, localised cavities that penetrate deep into the metal while leaving the surrounding surface—and often a covering paint layer—largely intact \cite{balangao2024corrosion}. A structure may therefore appear sound during routine visual inspection while pits propagate toward critical depths, eventually precipitating sudden failures such as pipeline leaks, loss of containment, or structural collapse. This concealed, non-linear failure mode makes pitting disproportionately dangerous relative to the small amount of material it removes.

The consequences of corrosion are severe and quantifiable. The direct global cost of corrosion is estimated at approximately USD~2.5 trillion annually—around 3.4\% of global GDP \cite{kahkesh2025algae, imran2025ship}, and the costs rise beyond USD~4 trillion when prevention, control, and productivity losses are included \cite{bakdash2026analytical}.

The oil and gas sector is disproportionately exposed: corrosion contributes to roughly 18\% of all significant pipeline incidents, and global expenditure on corrosion during maritime vessel operations alone approaches USD~5.2 billion per year \cite{olawole2026advanced}. Beyond the financial burden, corrosion-induced structural failures cause fatalities, fires, and environmental pollution, with corroding marine structures releasing heavy metals and toxic chemicals into surrounding ecosystems \cite{dalmora2025methods}.

Crucially, these losses are not inevitable: researchers estimate that effective early inspection and maintenance strategies can reduce corrosion-related expenses by 15--35\% \cite{olawole2026advanced}. Early and reliable detection of pitting corrosion is therefore paramount—identifying pits before they reach critical depth enables planned intervention, minimises repair costs, and prevents catastrophic failure. This motivates sustained research into automated corrosion detection for offshore integrity management.

A range of detection approaches currently exists in industry and the research literature, spanning visual (RGB) imaging, thermal infrared sensing, and three-dimensional laser or LiDAR profiling, increasingly coupled with machine learning. However, as Section~II details, these approaches are applied almost exclusively in isolation in the offshore domain, and each modality in isolation is fundamentally limited: RGB captures only surface appearance, thermal sensing has rarely been validated outside controlled industrial settings, and LiDAR quantifies geometry but cannot reveal subsurface anomalies. Whilst trimodal fusion of RGB, thermal, and laser profilometry has recently proven effective in controlled industrial environments \cite{zhang2026application}, no existing study applies this combination to pitting corrosion detection on offshore infrastructure.

This research proposes to close that gap by developing a machine learning framework that fuses RGB, thermal, and LiDAR imagery specifically for pitting corrosion detection in offshore infrastructure. The objectives are: (1)~to design a multi-sensor fusion architecture combining RGB, thermal, and LiDAR data streams; (2)~to develop a machine learning model for automated pitting corrosion detection using the fused modalities; (3)~to evaluate detection performance against single- and dual-modality baselines under offshore-representative conditions; and (4)~to validate the framework on corrosion datasets representative of marine and offshore environments.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{RGB/Visual Image-Based Corrosion Detection}

Visual corrosion detection has advanced rapidly through deep learning. Convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures—including AlexNet, VGG11, and VGG16—have been applied to hull structural plate corrosion classification, learning discriminative rust textures and colour irregularities \cite{yao2019artificial}. For pitting specifically, Soares et al.\ \cite{soares2023autonomous} applied U-Net segmentation to ship tank imagery and achieved an Intersection over Union (IoU) of 0.9453 with a mean squared error of 0.1639, substantially outperforming classical edge detectors on the characteristic deep-well morphology of pits. Alvani et al.\ \cite{alvani2026corrosion} combined VGG16 transfer learning with an XGBoost classification head, reaching 98.47\% accuracy and a 98.83\% F1-score on a 10{,}000-image gas pipeline dataset. Imran et al.\ \cite{imran2025ship} took a computationally lighter route, pairing a Chan--Vese Active Contour model with Wiener and Modified Gaussian Notch Reject filtering to achieve 98\% classification accuracy and 93.83\% segmentation accuracy on ship structures in near real-time.

Despite these strong benchmark results, RGB-only approaches degrade markedly in offshore conditions. Metal reflection and poor lighting in confined spaces such as ship tanks impede reliable detection \cite{soares2023autonomous}, while open maritime environments introduce motion blur, fog, salt spray, and reflections that CNN-based models generalise to poorly \cite{imran2025ship, yao2019artificial}. No current visual model adapts dynamically to changing environmental feedback such as water clarity or surface condition.

\subsection{Thermal/Infrared-Based Corrosion Detection}

Thermal sensing provides a capability RGB fundamentally lacks: access to subsurface information. Because corrosion alters a metal's material properties, damaged regions retain and release heat differently from healthy metal, producing temperature variations detectable by infrared thermography even when the corrosion is concealed beneath paint \cite{zhang2026application}. Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2026application} deployed Short-Wave Infrared (SWIR) thermal imagers with wavelet-based thermal noise removal as part of a multi-source inspection platform for boiler water-cooled wall tubes, contributing to an overall detection accuracy of 96.2\%.

However, thermal corrosion detection remains largely confined to controlled industrial and laboratory scenarios. In field deployment, interference from dust and water vapour blurs target regions and introduces noise \cite{zhang2026application}, and no current approach integrates thermal sensing into a real-time adaptive multimodal model capable of responding to the high salinity and fluctuating surface temperatures characteristic of offshore infrastructure.

\subsection{LiDAR and 3D Point Cloud-Based Inspection}

LiDAR and laser profilometry supply the geometric dimension that optical and thermal imaging cannot: three-dimensional quantification of corrosion morphology. Point cloud pipelines employing voxelisation and random downsampling balance computational cost against structural fidelity, enabling calculation of precise geometric indicators including maximum corrosion depth, average corrosion depth, and pit volume \cite{zhang2026application, olawole2026advanced}. In the marine domain, coloured 3D LiDAR point clouds have been used to detect and map corrosion spots across entire vessel hulls \cite{yu2022surface}.

The principal barriers are computational. High-resolution 3D processing carries a large memory footprint, and autonomous underwater vehicles operate under strict power and thermal constraints that preclude full onboard execution \cite{olawole2026advanced}. Most current systems consequently rely on external post-processing, leaving real-time onboard 3D profiling an open problem.

\subsection{Multi-Modal and Sensor Fusion Approaches}

Sensor fusion for corrosion detection is an emerging but domain-limited field. Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2026application} constructed the study closest to the present proposal: a multi-source heterogeneous platform combining HD industrial RGB cameras, SWIR thermal imagers, and laser profilometers—the technical equivalent of the RGB--thermal--LiDAR suite—achieving 96.2\% detection accuracy and precise volumetric pit characterisation on boiler tubes. In a different fusion direction, Yang et al.\ \cite{yang2026pipeline} fused acoustic signals with image texture features (LBP and HOG descriptors derived from Mel spectrograms), reaching 98.14\% accuracy in classifying pipeline corrosion states, while Olawole et al.\ \cite{olawole2026advanced} propose GAN-based fusion of Magnetic Flux Leakage and Ultrasonic Testing data to reconstruct pipeline wall-thickness maps.

These studies collectively prove that multi-sensor fusion outperforms single-modality detection—but their domains are boiler tubes, gas pipelines, and reinforced concrete, not offshore structures. None has been adapted to the dynamic offshore environment, where low feature contrast between rust, salt deposits, and sea spray renders single-sensor signals unreliable. Notably, where multiple sensors are used in maritime practice, integration typically amounts to manual side-by-side comparison rather than automated, simultaneous fusion. Imran et al.\ \cite{imran2025ship} explicitly identify this absence as a gap, recommending that future offshore research combine thermal and optical sensing to detect hidden corrosion.

\subsection{Research Gap}

The literature therefore presents a two-layer gap. First, a \textit{sensor fusion gap}: trimodal RGB--thermal--laser fusion is demonstrably effective for pitting detection and volumetric measurement \cite{zhang2026application}, but only within controlled industrial environments. Second, a \textit{domain specificity gap}: offshore and marine studies remain restricted to single or dual modalities—RGB alone \cite{soares2023autonomous, imran2025ship} or RGB with LiDAR \cite{yu2022surface}—none of which can simultaneously capture surface texture, subsurface thermal anomalies, and three-dimensional pit geometry. No existing study combines RGB, thermal, and LiDAR data for the identification and volumetric measurement of pitting corrosion on offshore assets. The proposed research directly addresses this intersection.
